Access to advanced computing is now treated as a strategic variable in its own right. Semiconductors, cloud capacity, model weights and the services that deliver them have all become objects of regulation, not merely inputs to broader defence or industrial policy. This research agenda examines how such access can be governed in practice, given that computing is distributed across international supply chains, rented rather than owned, and continually changing in cost and capability.

A central concern is the relationship between physical and non-physical forms of control. Export controls on chips and manufacturing equipment remain important, but they increasingly coexist with restrictions on cloud access, know-your-customer obligations for model providers, and due-diligence requirements for intermediaries. The technical and institutional challenge is to make these instruments complementary rather than contradictory, and to ensure that restrictions are targeted, verifiable and enforceable.

The agenda also addresses the feasibility of different governance designs. Can governments identify the location and end-use of high-end computing with sufficient confidence? What information can and should cloud and semiconductor providers be expected to collect? How can compliance obligations be proportionate for smaller actors while still constraining deliberate misuse? And what international coordination is necessary for controls to remain effective as models, chips and compute providers multiply across jurisdictions?

Finally, the work considers the roles of different public and private actors. Governments set the legal framework, but implementation depends on firms that design chips, operate clouds, train models and serve customers. The research explores how these actors can be incorporated into governance arrangements without placing unrealistic demands on any single party, and how accountability can be maintained as the locus of control shifts from traditional export licensing to service-based regulation.
