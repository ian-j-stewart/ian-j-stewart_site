Universities and research institutions are increasingly expected to manage the security risks associated with international collaboration. Government guidance has expanded, but the practical challenge falls on institutions that must interpret it, embed it in grant management, hiring, international partnerships and laboratory practice, and do so without undermining the openness that makes research productive. This agenda examines how that can be done.

A core concern is intangible technology transfer. Unlike physical export control, which tracks the movement of goods across borders, intangible transfer involves knowledge, skills, data and research relationships. Identifying where the boundary lies between legitimate scientific exchange and technology transfer of concern is difficult, and the institutional systems for doing so are still developing. The research asks what evidence is available about the scale and nature of the risk, and what governance instruments can address it without being indiscriminate.

Another strand examines the role of due diligence. Research institutions must assess collaborators, funders, end-uses and export-control implications, often with limited information and competing incentives. The work considers what due-diligence standards are feasible, how they can be integrated with existing compliance and ethics processes, and what support institutions need from government and the private sector.

The agenda also addresses the governance of emerging technologies. As artificial intelligence, biotechnology and quantum technologies mature, the categories used to define sensitive research may not keep pace. The research explores how governments, funding bodies and universities can develop adaptive governance frameworks that remain focused on genuine security risks while preserving the conditions for international scientific cooperation.
