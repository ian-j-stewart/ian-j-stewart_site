Export controls, sanctions, investment screening and technology-transfer restrictions have become central to how states compete over advanced technology. What was once a specialised nonproliferation and arms-control toolbox is now a routine instrument of economic statecraft, applied to semiconductors, artificial intelligence, biotechnology and other strategic sectors. This research agenda examines the design, implementation and international consequences of that shift.

A starting point is the relationship between diffusion and control. Technology is most effectively controlled when supplier states agree on the items, destinations and end-uses of concern. But strategic competition has made such agreement harder. Unilateral controls and extraterritorial measures can be effective in the short term, but they also create incentives for alternative suppliers, rerouted trade and indigenous development. The research asks how multilateral coordination can be sustained when the strategic priorities of major powers diverge.

Another strand focuses on implementation. Export controls are not only diplomatic instruments; they are also legal and operational requirements that firms, universities, banks and research institutions must translate into daily practice. Licensing, due diligence, classification, record-keeping and internal review all shape whether controls achieve their intended effect. The work examines where implementation succeeds and where it breaks down, and what institutional capacity is needed to close the gap between policy intent and practical outcome.

The agenda also considers the political economy of technology control. Measures that restrict trade in strategic goods can have wide economic effects, affecting allied industries, research collaboration and global supply chains. The research explores how governments can design controls that are targeted, proportionate and enforceable, and how they can align economic-security objectives with the longer-term health of innovation ecosystems.
