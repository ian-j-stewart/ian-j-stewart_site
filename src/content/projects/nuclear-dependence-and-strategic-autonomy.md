Nuclear weapons are often described in terms of national strategic autonomy, yet their production, maintenance and operation rely on international supply chains, allied support and shared technology. This research agenda examines the tension between the political language of independence and the practical reality of dependence. It is grounded in the United Kingdom's nuclear programme, but it speaks to wider questions about the nonproliferation regime, naval nuclear propulsion and the politics of alliance.

The United Kingdom's Trident programme illustrates the issue. The deterrent is formally independent, yet it draws on a shared missile pool with the United States, a domestic industrial base that depends on specialised supply chains, and a workforce and infrastructure that cannot be replicated quickly. AUKUS has added a new dimension: the prospective transfer of nuclear-powered submarine technology to Australia raises questions about safeguards, naval fuel and the conditions under which such cooperation is sustainable.

A second strand examines how other states seek to manage similar dependencies. Brazil's nuclear-powered submarine programme and its request for safeguards exceptions, for example, show how naval propulsion can become a site of contest between national ambition and nonproliferation obligations. The research asks how such cases should be assessed, and what standards of transparency and verification are necessary if similar programmes proliferate.

The agenda also addresses the institutional side of the problem. The IAEA safeguards system was designed for a world in which sensitive nuclear activities were more concentrated and more visible. New naval propulsion arrangements, fuel-cycle partnerships and national reprocessing programmes test that system. The research explores how safeguards, export controls and supplier-state coordination might adapt without undermining the credibility of the nonproliferation regime.
