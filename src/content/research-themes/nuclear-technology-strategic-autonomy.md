My work on nuclear technology and strategic autonomy examines how technological dependence, international cooperation and national capabilities shape claims of strategic autonomy in the nuclear field. The United Kingdom's nuclear deterrent is a longstanding case, but the issues are broader: they include the IAEA safeguards system, the Nuclear Suppliers Group, national enrichment and reprocessing programmes, and the political significance of nuclear-powered submarines.

A recurring theme is the tension between the desire for national independence and the practical reality of international supply chains. Few states can produce all of the technology, material and know-how needed for a nuclear programme alone. This dependence creates both vulnerabilities and opportunities for cooperation, and it shapes how states negotiate export controls, safeguards and alliance relationships.

Current work in this area focuses on the United Kingdom's deterrent and its technological and operational dependencies, the nonproliferation implications of advanced naval propulsion programmes, and the changing role of the IAEA and other institutions in verifying and governing nuclear technology.
