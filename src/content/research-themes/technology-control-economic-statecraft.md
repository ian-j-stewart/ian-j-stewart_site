A central strand of my work examines how states translate strategic objectives into practical instruments of technology control. Export controls, sanctions, foreign-investment screening and related measures have become central tools of economic statecraft, but their effectiveness depends on implementation: licensing decisions, compliance practices, due diligence, inter-agency coordination and the behaviour of the private actors that actually move goods, money and knowledge across borders.

I am interested both in how these regimes are designed and in how they work on the ground. This includes the evolution of the multilateral export-control system, the role of the Nuclear Suppliers Group and other coordination forums, the increasing use of unilateral and extraterritorial measures, and the emergence of new mechanisms for controlling intangible technology, services and data.

The research has direct relevance for policymakers, regulators, compliance professionals and the research institutions affected by these rules. It also connects to broader debates about the relationship between national security and economic interdependence, and about whether the existing trade-control architecture is fit for an era of strategic competition.
