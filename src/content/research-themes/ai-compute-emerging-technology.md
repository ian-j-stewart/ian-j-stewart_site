Advances in artificial intelligence, advanced semiconductors and cloud computing are reshaping the relationship between technology and international security. My research in this area examines how access to these capabilities can be controlled, how they are being used to support security analysis and decision-making, and what governance arrangements are needed to manage their risks.

A particular focus is compute governance: the idea that access to the computing infrastructure needed to train and run advanced AI models is itself a strategic object of regulation. This includes export controls on semiconductors and manufacturing equipment, restrictions on cloud access, know-your-customer and due-diligence obligations, and the development of technical and institutional capacity to verify compliance.

I am also interested in how AI can be used responsibly for open-source analysis, nonproliferation research and decision-support. This requires attention to provenance, uncertainty, bias, auditability and human accountability. The goal is not to replace expert judgement but to support it, and to ensure that new analytical tools are introduced in ways that are transparent and defensible.
